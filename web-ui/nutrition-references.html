Understanding Your Nutrition Recommendations

What This Guide Explains

This guide helps you understand the science behind the nutrition recommendations you receive. All recommendations are based on peer-reviewed research from leading health organizations and scientific journals.

How We Calculate Your Daily Needs

Your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)

Your BMR is the number of calories your body burns at rest just to keep you alive (breathing, circulating blood, maintaining body temperature, etc.). We calculate this using the Mifflin-St Jeor Equation, which is the most accurate formula validated by scientific research.

Reference: Mifflin, M.D., St Jeor, S.T., Hill, L.A., Scott, B.J., Daugherty, S.A. and Koh, Y.O. (1990) 'A new predictive equation for resting energy expenditure in healthy individuals', The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 51(2), pp. 241-247.

Activity Level Adjustments

We multiply your BMR by an activity factor based on your daily movement:

  • Sedentary (little to no exercise): 1.2×
  • Lightly active (light exercise 1-3 days/week): 1.375×
  • Moderately active (moderate exercise 3-5 days/week): 1.55×
  • Very active (hard exercise 6-7 days/week): 1.725×
  • Athlete (very hard exercise & physical job): 1.9×
Reference: FAO/WHO/UNU (2004) Human energy requirements: Report of a Joint FAO/WHO/UNU Expert Consultation. Rome: Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations.

Macronutrients: Your Energy Sources

Carbohydrates (45-65% of daily calories)

Carbohydrates are your body's preferred energy source. This range ensures you get enough energy while reducing the risk of chronic diseases like diabetes and heart disease.

Reference: Institute of Medicine (2005) Dietary reference intakes for energy, carbohydrate, fiber, fat, fatty acids, cholesterol, protein, and amino acids. Washington, DC: The National Academies Press.

Protein (10-35% of daily calories)

Protein builds and repairs tissues, makes enzymes and hormones, and supports immune function. This range maintains nitrogen balance and supports muscle health.

Special considerations:

  • Weight loss: Higher protein (1.4× normal) helps preserve muscle mass and increases satiety
  • Muscle gain: Increased protein (1.3× normal) supports muscle growth
  • Athletes: Extra protein (1.2× normal) aids recovery and adaptation
References:
  • Institute of Medicine (2005) - Base recommendations
  • Westerterp-Plantenga, M.S., Lemmens, S.G. and Westerterp, K.R. (2012) 'Dietary protein–its role in satiety, energetics, weight loss and health', British Journal of Nutrition, 108(S2), pp. S105-S112.
  • Phillips, S.M. and Van Loon, L.J. (2011) 'Dietary protein for athletes: from requirements to optimum adaptation', Journal of Sports Sciences, 29(sup1), pp. S29-S38.

Fat (20-35% of daily calories)

Fats are essential for absorbing vitamins A, D, E, and K, producing hormones, and providing energy. This range ensures adequate intake of essential fatty acids.

Important limits:

  • Saturated fat: Keep under 10% of calories to reduce heart disease risk
  • Trans fats: Avoid completely - they increase bad cholesterol and heart disease risk
References:
  • Institute of Medicine (2005) - Macronutrient recommendations
  • U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and U.S. Department of Agriculture (2020) Dietary guidelines for Americans, 2020-2025. 9th edn.

Dietary Fiber

Fiber promotes digestive health, helps control blood sugar, lowers cholesterol, and aids weight management.

Daily targets:

  • Men (19-50 years): 30-38g
  • Women (19-50 years): 25g
  • Men (over 50): 30g
  • Women (over 50): 21g

The recommendation is based on 14g of fiber per 1,000 calories consumed, which has been shown to reduce heart disease risk.

Reference: Institute of Medicine (2005) - Fiber and cardiovascular health

Hydration

Water and Fluid Intake

Proper hydration is essential for every body function. These recommendations include water from all beverages and moisture from food.

Daily targets:

  • Men: 3.7 liters (about 15.5 cups)
  • Women: 2.7 liters (about 11.5 cups)
Reference: Institute of Medicine (2005) Dietary reference intakes for water, potassium, sodium, chloride, and sulfate. Washington, DC: The National Academies Press.

Important Minerals

Sodium (1,500mg/day)

Sodium is essential for nerve function and fluid balance, but excess intake raises blood pressure. The recommended amount supports health without increasing hypertension risk.

Potassium (4,700mg/day)

Potassium helps lower blood pressure, reduces the negative effects of sodium, and decreases kidney stone risk. Found in bananas, potatoes, leafy greens, and beans.

Iron

Iron carries oxygen in your blood. Needs vary by gender and age:

  • Men: 8mg/day
  • Women (19-50 years): 18mg/day (higher due to menstrual blood loss)
  • Women (over 50): 8mg/day

Calcium

Critical for bone health and muscle function:

  • Adults (19-50 years): 1,000mg/day
  • Men (51-70 years): 1,000mg/day
  • Women (51-70 years): 1,200mg/day
  • Adults (over 70): 1,200mg/day
Reference: Institute of Medicine (2011) Dietary reference intakes for calcium and vitamin D. Washington, DC: The National Academies Press.

Magnesium

Supports over 300 body processes including energy production, muscle function, and bone health:

  • Men (19-30 years): 400mg/day
  • Men (31+ years): 420mg/day
  • Women (19-30 years): 310mg/day
  • Women (31+ years): 320mg/day

Zinc

Essential for immune function, wound healing, and DNA synthesis:

  • Men: 11mg/day
  • Women: 8mg/day
Reference for minerals: Institute of Medicine (2001) Dietary reference intakes for vitamin A, vitamin K, arsenic, boron, chromium, copper, iodine, iron, manganese, molybdenum, nickel, silicon, vanadium, and zinc. Washington, DC: The National Academies Press.

Vitamins

Vitamin A (700-900mcg/day)

Essential for vision, immune function, and skin health. Men need 900mcg, women need 700mcg.

B Vitamins

The B vitamin family supports energy production, brain function, and red blood cell formation:

  • Thiamin (B1): Men 1.2mg, Women 1.1mg - Prevents beriberi, supports nerve function
  • Riboflavin (B2): Men 1.3mg, Women 1.1mg - Energy metabolism
  • Niacin (B3): Men 16mg, Women 14mg - Prevents pellagra, supports skin health
  • Vitamin B6: 1.3-1.7mg depending on age - Supports brain health
  • Vitamin B12: 2.4mcg - Critical for nerve function and red blood cells
  • Folate: 400mcg - Prevents birth defects, maintains healthy blood cells
  • Choline: Men 550mg, Women 425mg - Brain and liver health
Reference: Institute of Medicine (1998) Dietary reference intakes for thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, vitamin B6, folate, vitamin B12, pantothenic acid, biotin, and choline. Washington, DC: The National Academies Press.

Vitamin C

Powerful antioxidant that supports immune function, wound healing, and collagen production:

  • Men: 90mg/day
  • Women: 75mg/day

Vitamin D

Critical for calcium absorption and bone health:

  • Adults (19-70 years): 600 IU (15mcg)/day
  • Adults (over 70): 800 IU (20mcg)/day
Reference: Institute of Medicine (2011) - Vitamin D and bone health

Vitamin E (15mg/day)

Antioxidant that protects cells from damage and supports immune function.

Vitamin K

Essential for blood clotting and bone health:

  • Men: 120mcg/day
  • Women: 90mcg/day
Reference for vitamins: Institute of Medicine (2000) Dietary reference intakes for vitamin C, vitamin E, selenium, and carotenoids. Washington, DC: The National Academies Press.

Weight Management

For Weight Loss

Calorie reduction: 500 calories below your maintenance level per day

  • Results in approximately 0.5kg (1 pound) weight loss per week
  • Safe, sustainable rate supported by obesity research

Protein increase: 40% more protein than normal

  • Helps preserve muscle mass during weight loss
  • Increases feelings of fullness
  • Boosts metabolism slightly

Fiber increase: 30% more fiber

  • Increases satiety and reduces hunger
  • Helps control blood sugar
  • Promotes digestive health
References:
  • Hall, K.D. and Kahan, S. (2018) 'Maintenance of lost weight and long-term management of obesity', Medical Clinics of North America, 102(1), pp. 183-197.
  • Westerterp-Plantenga, M.S., et al. (2012) - Protein and satiety
  • Howarth, N.C., Saltzman, E. and Roberts, S.B. (2001) 'Dietary fiber and weight regulation', Nutrition Reviews, 59(5), pp. 129-139.

For Weight Gain

Calorie increase: 300 calories above your maintenance level per day

  • Promotes muscle gain while minimizing fat accumulation
  • Sustainable approach for lean mass development

Protein increase: 30% more protein

  • Supports muscle protein synthesis
  • Optimal for strength training adaptations
Reference: Garthe, I., Raastad, T., Refsnes, P.E., Koivisto, A. and Sundgot-Borgen, J. (2011) 'Effect of two different weight-gain diets on body composition and strength gains in elite athletes', International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism, 21(2), pp. 97-104.

Special Considerations for Athletes

Athletes have increased nutrient needs due to training demands, increased muscle mass, and higher metabolism. Adjustments include:

  • Protein: 20% increase - supports muscle repair and adaptation
  • Vitamin C: 20% increase - enhanced immune function and recovery
  • Vitamin E: 10% increase - protects against exercise-induced oxidative stress
  • Magnesium: 10% increase - supports energy production and muscle function
  • Zinc: 10% increase - aids recovery and immune function
  • Water: 20% increase - replaces fluid lost through sweat
Reference: Thomas, D.T., Erdman, K.A. and Burke, L.M. (2016) 'Position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada, and the American College of Sports Medicine: nutrition and athletic performance', Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 116(3), pp. 501-528.

Why These Sources Matter

All our recommendations come from:

Institute of Medicine (IOM) / National Academies

The gold standard for nutrition science in the United States. Their Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs) are based on:

  • Comprehensive reviews of thousands of scientific studies
  • Analysis by expert panels of leading nutrition scientists
  • Consideration of both deficiency prevention and chronic disease risk

FAO/WHO/UNU

International nutrition standards representing global consensus from the:

  • Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)
  • World Health Organization (WHO)
  • United Nations University (UNU)

U.S. Dietary Guidelines

Updated every 5 years by the U.S. government based on the latest scientific evidence, reviewed by independent advisory committees.

Understanding the Terms

  • RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance): The average daily intake sufficient to meet nutrient requirements of 97-98% of healthy individuals
  • AI (Adequate Intake): Used when RDA cannot be determined; based on observed or experimentally determined intake levels
  • AMDR (Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range): Percentage ranges of calories from carbs, protein, and fat associated with reduced chronic disease risk
  • UL (Tolerable Upper Intake Level): Maximum daily intake unlikely to cause adverse health effects

How to Use These Recommendations

  1. Personalized for You: Our system calculates your specific needs based on your age, gender, weight, height, activity level, and goals
  2. Evidence-Based: Every recommendation traces back to peer-reviewed research
  3. Flexible Guidelines: These are targets to aim for over time, not rigid rules for every single day
  4. Consult Professionals: For specific health conditions or concerns, always consult with a registered dietitian or healthcare provider
